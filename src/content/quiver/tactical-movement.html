Tactical movement is moving a team under combat conditions when it is not yet in contact: getting to the right place, at the right time, ready to fight, without being found on the way. Movement ends when contact is made or the objective is reached. Movement is not manoeuvre; manoeuvre starts once the enemy is found.

Fundamentals

  • Avoid all contact. Contact is made at the time and place of our choosing.
  • Cohesion: stay together.
  • Communications.
  • Momentum: not late, not early.
  • Security, all round, all the time.
  • When contact is unavoidable, switch to manoeuvre fast and with the smallest element in contact.

To keep surprise and the initiative, a team moving in enemy ground:

  • Avoids chance contact where it can.
  • Moves on covered and concealed routes.
  • Avoids likely ambush sites and other danger areas.
  • Keeps camouflage, noise and light discipline.
  • Keeps 360-degree security.
  • Makes contact with the smallest element if contact cannot be avoided.
  • Keeps the initiative to attack at the time and place of its choosing.

Movement techniques

Three techniques, picked by how likely contact is. Combined with a formation they cover any situation, mounted or on foot.

Traveling

Contact is not expected. The team moves as one, close enough to control, fastest of the three.

Traveling overwatch

Contact is possible. The lead element goes ahead far enough that if it is hit, the rest are out of the same fire and can support it.

Bounding overwatch

Contact is likely or expected. One element sits in a position it can shoot from while the other moves; then they swap. Slowest and most secure.

Route and navigation

Picking the route is a leader's skill. A good route adds security, saves effort, and delivers the team on time and ready to fight. It starts with reading the terrain and ends with good navigation; the best plan is worthless if the team cannot find the objective, or worse, walks onto it.

Route selection

Read the terrain twice. First for tactical advantage, ours and theirs: ground that dominates the approach is key terrain and makes good intermediate objectives. Second for navigation: what will keep us on track. If you want to avoid contact, pick ground that hides the team. If you want to make contact, pick ground you can observe from. Most of the route should give some advantage.

  • Use the GPS and the cTab: waypoints make the route easier to plan and to follow.
  • Avoid built-up areas and anywhere with traffic.

Route planning

A route is made of enabling tasks, some or all of: GPS waypoints, passage of friendly lines, movement to an objective rally point, to a phase line, to a limit of advance, link-up with another team, movement to a patrol base, and re-entry of friendly lines.

Start at the end. Pick the objective or limit of advance, then work back to where you are, marking everything that matters on the way: navigation aids, good positions, known and likely enemy, control measures. Break the route into legs and sketch it. Three decisions per leg: the kind of navigation, the kind of route, and where the leg starts and ends.

Develop a leg

A leg has one distance and one direction; a change of direction ends it. Each leg starts and ends at a control measure, a checkpoint or phase line, captured as a GPS waypoint, and ideally on a navigation aid such as a catching feature or an attack point. For each leg: pick the navigation and route type, measure distance and direction, note what matters for that leg, mark the map. Then ask two questions. If contact is made here, are we at an advantage? Can we get out?

Emergency exfil

The direction the team escapes and evades in if it all goes wrong. Each team gets its own, so the enemy cannot guess it.

Navigation aids

Linear aids are trails, streams, ridgelines, wood lines, power lines, streets and contour lines; point aids are hilltops and prominent buildings. Linear features get phase lines, point features get checkpoints or rally points. Three kinds of aid:

  • Catching features: an obvious feature beyond the checkpoint or objective. Reach it and you know you have gone too far.
  • Handrails: a linear feature parallel to the route. Follow it to stay oriented and move faster; it doubles as a catching feature.
  • Navigational attack points: an obvious landmark near the objective or checkpoint. From there the team switches from rough navigation to precise.

Two tricks with them. Offset compass: if you might miss the end point, deliberately aim left or right of it at a catching feature, then turn and walk in; best when the feature is linear. Boxing in: use catching features, handrails and attack points as the four sides of a box round the leg, so any drift is caught and corrected.

Route types

Three kinds of route. Terrain association works with all of them; the general azimuth method with the first two; point navigation with the third.

  • Terrain feature: follow a ridge, a valley, a street. Least tiring, but it follows natural lines of drift, which is where chance contact happens.
  • Contouring: stay at one height round a hill or along a ridge. No needless climbing, and the contour is its own handrail and catching feature. This is our preferred method in Arma: by its nature it leads the team round danger areas.
  • Cross compartment: follow an azimuth straight across the terrain. The most direct, but tiring and exposed.

Execution

On the move the leader runs the plan, keeps an accurate location at all times, and designates rally points. Without a location the team gets no fires, no reserve and no help.

Common skills

Everyone, and leaders above all, must be good at land navigation: a grid, a compass by day and night, resection and intersection, reading terrain, measuring distance and elevation, the GPS and the cTab. The Land navigation page has the detail.

Rate of movement and stealth

Set a pace the team can keep and that gets it there on time. As fast as possible is seldom right. Progress matters, but speed is never a substitute for security. If stamina is on, the slower you move the more you keep for the fight.

Counter-tracking

Assume someone is following and make it hard for them.

  • Change direction often, at least every 1,000 metres, and cover the change, so nobody following can guess the destination and leapfrog you. Cross a road or track at an off-angle to your azimuth; a short way past it, turn sharply and cover the sign. Straight lines are bad.
  • Loop back: cross your own trail during the move to check nobody is on it.

Rally points

A rally point is where the team reassembles and reorganises if it is split up, or halts briefly to prepare for the objective, to leave friendly lines or to come back in. Planned rally points: the objective rally point, the initial rally point, the re-entry rally point. Unplanned: en-route, near-side and far-side rally points. The actions at each are the same.

Danger areas

A danger area is any ground on the route where the enemy could see or shoot the team. Plan round them; when you cannot, cross as quickly and carefully as possible, with near-side and far-side rally points set beforehand. For an unexpected one, the nearest en-route rally points serve.

  • Open areas: hide on the near side and observe, post security, send an element to clear the far side, then cross the rest at the shortest exposed distance, fast.
  • Roads and trails: cross at a bend, a narrow spot or low ground.
  • Villages: pass downwind and well clear. Avoid animals, especially dogs.
  • Enemy positions: pass downwind; watch for trip wires and warning devices.
  • Minefields: bypass, even at the cost of a long detour. Breach only when there is no other way.
  • Streams: a narrow spot with concealment on both banks, observe the far side, security on both sides, clear the far bank, then cross fast and quiet.
  • Wire obstacles: avoid them; the enemy covers them with observation and fire.

Crossing a danger area

  1. The lead element signals "danger area", relayed back; the team halts. The leader comes forward, confirms it, and picks the technique.
  2. The leader sets near-side and far-side rally points.
  3. Near-side security watches the flanks and overwatches the crossing.
  4. With near-side security in place, the leader sends the far-side security element across.
  5. The far-side element clears the far side, posts security on the flanks and overwatches, then signals clear.
  6. The team crosses, fast and quiet.

The same steps apply to a single team crossing on its own.

Large open areas

Too large to go round in the time available: cross with traveling overwatch to save time, switching to bounding overwatch wherever contact may be expected and once within small-arms range of the far side, about 250 metres. Re-form beyond it and carry on.

Small open areas

Small enough to go round. Two ways:

  • Contour round it: set a rally point on the far side on the movement azimuth, pick the side with the better distance, cover and concealment, and go round inside the wood line. At the rally point, pick the azimuth up again.
  • Detour bypass: turn 90 degrees left or right, move until you are past it, turn back onto the direction of travel, then close the box with another 90-degree turn to the far-side rally point. The offset legs do not count toward the pace count of the route.

Contact at a danger area

If the team is hit in or near a danger area it moves to the rally points; the leader picks near or far side by where the contact came from. In the dark, lasers can point out the rally point at a distance, and the first element there marks it with IR so the rest can find it. Position updates on the move let separated elements find each other and tell friend from foe.

Security and protection

Security is the constant theme of movement. It needs everyone concentrating on the enemy, which only happens when the basics of movement are second nature; a team still thinking about the basics is not thinking about the enemy. Security comes from covered and concealed ground, the right formation and technique, the drills at danger areas, noise, light and radio discipline, and personal camouflage. Before the move, the leader works out the known and likely enemy positions and what they might do, and picks the security measures from that.

The enemy

Aim for stealth and avoid contact, but expect it the whole way. Avoid routes with obvious danger areas: built-up areas, roads, trails, known enemy positions. Where they cannot be avoided, manage the risk. For stealth, stay away from locals, towns and lines of drift.

The techniques set how much security the team has: traveling the least, when contact is not likely; traveling overwatch when it is likely but not imminent; bounding overwatch when it is imminent. The leader sets a probable line of deployment where the switch from traveling overwatch to bounding overwatch happens. In contact, movement becomes manoeuvre, fire and movement, while the leader runs the actions on contact.

Risk management

Risk is always there; the aim is to remove the unnecessary risk, not all of it. It is part of planning at every level and in every phase.

  1. Identify hazards: anything that can injure people, lose equipment or degrade the mission, across every phase. Start in planning, keep going through the operation.
  2. Assess them: which can be removed or avoided; for the rest, how likely and how bad; from those, the risk level.
  3. Control them: brief every hazard and its control; thorough pre-combat checks; time to rehearse; buddy teams; vehicle and aviation discipline; clear visual signals and markers between manoeuvring elements; a way of bringing new people in.

Fratricide

Friendly weapons killing or injuring friendly people. Preventing it is the leader's job. Everyone must know where they are and where the other friendly elements are, anticipate the dangerous moments, and keep positions reported so the rest know too.

  • Effects: lost confidence in leaders, hesitation with supporting fires, over-supervision, lost aggression and initiative, disrupted operations, a weaker team.
  • Causes: no positive identification and lost situational awareness above all; failures in the fire control plan, in navigation, in combat identification, in control measures, in reporting; individual and weapon errors; battlefield hazards; over-reliance on instruments.
  • Prevention: find and assess the fratricide risks in the planning; keep situational understanding; positive target identification every time; effective fire control.

Contact during movement

On contact the element in contact and the leader decide fast how to react.

Hasty attack: you see them first

A smaller enemy, unready and moving toward you in traveling order, is engaged only when contact cannot be avoided. The team has a short time to set a hasty ambush. Only against a force not much bigger than yours and in traveling order: the leader sets the team so fire is concentrated on the enemy as they pass through.

Ambush: they see you first

An ambush is a surprise attack from a concealed position.

  • Near ambush: get out of the kill zone now, toward the attackers, and through their line.
  • Far ambush: take cover, return fire, indirect if there is any, or break contact.

Human nature in an ambush is to go to ground, and that is what the enemy wants: they chose a kill zone with the least cover. A team that goes to ground in it stays in it and dies in it. The only way out is immediate rapid fire toward the ambush and an assault with all possible speed and violence: win the firefight and get out of the kill zone, taking the fight to the enemy. Elements in the kill zone move through it at speed, firing at the highest rate they can, toward the enemy or the shortest way out; they do not stop. Elements outside the kill zone manoeuvre at once to suppress the ambushers, or to attack them if there is a chance. This must be drilled until it is a reflex.

In an ambush there is no time for anything but survival: fire superiority and out of the kill zone. Hesitating or taking cover destroys the team.

Meeting engagement: at the same time

A moving team, not deployed for battle, meets the enemy at an unexpected time and place.

  1. Fire superiority, and keep rear security.
  2. Manoeuvre.
  3. Clear and search.
  4. Move off quickly on a different direction of march.
  5. If anything suggests the team was expected: warn everyone, destroy mission material, exfil on the emergency direction.

Break contact

A small team breaks contact as soon as it can; it cannot stay and fight. It fires and moves in groups of two or three, uses frag and smoke to cover the withdrawal, and keeps bounding until contact is broken, then consolidates at a rally point and reorganises.

Principles

  • A high initial volume of fire to kill or suppress.
  • Smoke to screen the move.
  • Claymores dual-primed, command and timed, ready to go out at once; this can be part of the plan to abandon rucks.
  • Rehearsed "man down" actions.
  • Rehearsed "RTO down" actions to recover the radio.

Initial contact

On contact everyone takes cover and concealment without masking anyone else's fire. The lead element, the first three, deploys within a few steps of where they are and lays down a base of fire. The trail element deploys on the assistant team leader's call, left or right, by where the contact is and where the lead element went.

Reactions

Air attack

The first to hear or see an aircraft calls "red air" and the direction. The team moves fast into a line, well spread, at right angles to the aircraft's line of flight, and goes prone in whatever cover there is as each comes on line. Between passes, find better cover. To leave the area the leader gives a clock direction and distance; afterwards the team consolidates and moves to the last rally point. Engage aircraft only as a last resort, head on, with massed fire: slow aircraft at 50 metres, fast at 200. Another option is to split into two or three groups on a rally point and link up there.

Indirect fire

Deploy and take cover. If more rounds land, the leader gives a clock direction and distance to move; the team consolidates on the move or at the given distance, then moves off fast. The enemy may adjust onto the move, so stay oriented on 12 o'clock, and go to the last rally point or wherever the leader says.

Flares

  • Ground flare: move out of the lit area and take cover; close the firing eye to keep night vision. The leader picks the next direction.
  • Overhead flare with warning: prone, behind concealment if there is any, before it bursts; firing eye closed.
  • Overhead flare without warning: prone, into the nearest cover, concealment and shadow, until it burns out; firing eye closed.
